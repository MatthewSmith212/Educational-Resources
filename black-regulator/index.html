Self-oscillating buck converter · two transistors, no IC, no clock

The Black RegulatorHow Roman Black's two-transistor switch-mode supply turns 14 V into 5 V, explained down to the last electron.

Two cheap transistors, a zener, an inductor and a handful of capacitors and resistors make a switching regulator that oscillates by itself, needs no timing chip, and wastes far less power than a linear regulator would. This page explains exactly how, first in one continuous argument, then through every question a newcomer is likely to ask.

Part I · 1

The short version

Q1 is a switch between the 14 V supply and an inductor, L1. When Q1 is on, current builds up in L1 and flows on into the output capacitor and the load, so the output voltage rises. When Q1 is off, the inductor keeps the current flowing anyway, pulling it up from ground through the diode D1, so the output slowly falls. Switching Q1 on and off quickly, and choosing the right fraction of time it spends on, holds the output at an average of 5 V. That part is an ordinary buck converter.

What makes this circuit unusual is how it decides when to switch. Q2 has its base held at a fixed reference by the zener diode (node Z, about 5.6 V) and its emitter connected straight to the output. Q2 therefore compares the output with the reference. It conducts when the output is more than about 0.6 V below the zener voltage, and stops conducting when the output climbs past that point. Q2 conducting pulls base current out of Q1 through R2, which turns Q1 on. So: output low → Q2 on → Q1 on → output rises → Q2 off → Q1 off → output falls → and around again.

A comparator built from one transistor is slow and soft, and on its own would let Q1 hover half-on, getting hot. Capacitor C2 fixes that. It connects the switching node A (which swings the full 14 V every time Q1 switches) back to Q2's base. When Q1 begins to turn off, A falls, C2 drags Q2's base down with it, and Q2 is turned off harder: positive feedback, which makes the switching snap rather than drift. The same kick leaves node Z sitting about a volt below the zener voltage, and it takes a few microseconds for RZ to recharge C1 and C2 back up. Until it does, Q2 cannot turn on again no matter what the output does. That built-in pause is the off-time, and it sets the switching frequency.

The whole circuit in one sentence

A transistor comparator (Q2) watches the output against a zener reference and drives a transistor switch (Q1) into an LC filter, while a small capacitor (C2) feeds each switching edge back to the comparator so that every transition snaps cleanly and every off period lasts a predictable few microseconds.

Everything below elaborates on those three paragraphs. If you only want the mechanism, read Part I. If something in it raised a question, Part II almost certainly answers it.

About the numbers on this page

The original drawings name the transistors and show the topology but print no resistor, capacitor or inductor values. To make the explanation concrete, this page uses one consistent representative set: RZ = 1 kΩ, zener 5.6 V, C1 = 47 nF, C2 = 4.7 nF, R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 470 Ω, L1 = 220 µH, CLOAD = 220 µF, D1 a 1N5819 Schottky, and a 20 Ω load (250 mA). They are chosen to be sensible and to make the arithmetic easy to follow. They are not a transcription of the original build. The worked design example shows how each one was picked, so you can pick your own.

    The five nodes worth naming

    A node is a set of wires that are all connected together and so all share one voltage. The original drawings label three of them; two more are worth naming.

    • +14 V: the supply rail. Q1's emitter, R1 and RZ all hang from it.
    • Z: the reference node. Zener cathode, top of C1, left side of C2, bottom of RZ, and Q2's base. Normally clamped at the zener voltage; briefly knocked below it after every turn-off.
    • A: the switch node. Q1's collector, D1's cathode, the left end of L1, and the right side of C2. It is the only node that swings across the whole supply range: about +13.8 V with Q1 on, about −0.35 V with Q1 off.
    • B: the output. Right end of L1, CLOAD, the load, and Q2's emitter. About 5 V, with a few tens of millivolts of ripple.
    • Q1's base (the junction of R1 and R2): either at +14 V (Q1 off, nothing flowing in R1) or at about +13.2 V (Q1 on, its base-emitter junction forward biased).

    Part I · 3

    One cycle in four phases

    The original author drew the cycle as four snapshots. Here they are again, each with what is happening, why, and what every node is doing. Two phases (On, Off) last microseconds; the other two (Turn-off, Turn-on) are the fast transitions between them and last tens to hundreds of nanoseconds.

    PHASE 1 · ON · lasts about 4.5 µs

    Q1 is on and the inductor current climbs

    Q2's base sits at 5.6 V (held there by the zener) and its emitter is at the output, a little under 5 V. That is more than the ~0.6 V a silicon base-emitter junction needs, so Q2 conducts. Its collector current has to come from somewhere: it flows from the +14 V rail, out through Q1's emitter and base, down through R2, through Q2 and into the output. That is the base current path in the drawing. Current flowing out of a PNP transistor's base turns it on, so Q1 saturates and connects node A almost directly to +14 V.

    With 13.8 V on one end of L1 and 5 V on the other, the inductor has about 8.8 V across it. An inductor responds to a steady voltage with a steadily rising current: dI/dt = V/L = 8.8 V / 220 µH = 0.04 A per microsecond. That is the major current path. Once the inductor current exceeds what the load is drawing, the surplus charges CLOAD and the output voltage creeps up.

    Meanwhile RZ carries about 8.4 mA from the supply to node Z. A tiny part (well under a milliamp) goes into Q2's base (the regulator current path); the rest flows down through the zener, which is what holds Z at 5.6 V.

    PHASE 2 · TURN-OFF · a fraction of a microsecond

    The output crosses the threshold and the circuit snaps off

    The output has risen to the point where V(Z) − V(B) has shrunk below about 0.62 V. Q2 starts to conduct less. Less current is pulled out of Q1's base, R1 starts winning, and Q1 begins to turn off. Q1 is saturated, so there is a short storage time (a few hundred nanoseconds) before its collector actually lets go.

    When it does, the inductor refuses to let its current stop. To keep it flowing, the inductor drives node A downward until something conducts: that something is D1, which turns on as soon as A falls about 0.35 V below ground. Node A has just dropped by roughly 14 V in a few tens of nanoseconds.

    Here is the trick. C2 connects A to Z. A capacitor's voltage cannot change instantly, so a sudden 14 V drop on one side tries to pull the other side down by the same amount. Z is also connected to C1 (ten times bigger), so the drop is shared between them: C1 discharges into C2, exactly as the drawing says, and Z falls by 14.15 V × C2/(C1+C2) = 14.15 × 4.7/51.7 ≈ 1.3 V, to about 4.3 V. Q2's base is now about 0.7 V below its emitter. Q2 is not just off, it is reverse biased.

    This is positive feedback: Q2 beginning to turn off causes A to fall, and A falling turns Q2 off harder. The transition therefore completes on its own and very quickly, with no lingering half-on state.

    PHASE 3 · OFF · lasts about 7.3 µs

    The inductor freewheels while RZ slowly resets the trap

    Q1 is off. The inductor current now circulates through the major current path loop: up from ground through D1, through L1, into CLOAD and the load, and back to ground. The inductor has −0.35 V on one end and +5 V on the other, so its current falls at about 5.35 V / 220 µH ≈ 0.024 A per microsecond. Once it drops below the load current, CLOAD makes up the difference and the output voltage sags.

    At the same time, RZ (with roughly 9.5 mA through it, since Z is now lower) is recharging C1 and C2 back toward the zener voltage. These are the two charging paths in the drawing. Notice there is no zener current in this phase: Z is below 5.6 V, so the zener is not conducting at all. Every bit of RZ's current goes into the capacitors.

    This is why the original calls it the delay period. Q2 cannot turn on again until V(Z) − V(B) climbs back above about 0.62 V. The output does fall a little during this phase, but only by tens of millivolts. Z has to rise by about 1.3 V. So in practice it is Z's recovery, a simple RC charge, that decides when the off period ends. With the representative values it takes about 7.3 µs.

    PHASE 4 · TURN-ON · around a hundred nanoseconds

    Z recovers, Q2 conducts, and the circuit snaps on

    Z has climbed back far enough (and the output has sagged far enough) that Q2's base-emitter junction reaches about 0.62 V. Q2 begins to conduct, pulls current from Q1's base through R2, and Q1 starts to turn on. Unsaturated transistors turn on much faster than saturated ones turn off, so this edge is quicker than the turn-off edge.

    As Q1 conducts, node A leaps from −0.35 V to +13.8 V. D1 is now reverse biased and turns off. C2 pushes that rising edge into node Z: the C2 charge current in the drawing. It tries to push Z upward by 1.3 V, which turns Q2 on even harder (positive feedback again, this time in the on direction). Z cannot actually rise much, because Z is already close to 5.6 V: the surge is absorbed by the zener and by Q2's own base-emitter junction, which is why the drawing shows the C2 current splitting between those two paths.

    The circuit is now back in phase 1, with Z clamped at the zener voltage, and the cycle repeats. With the representative values, one full cycle takes about 11.8 µs, a frequency of about 85 kHz.

    Part I · 4

    Why the output lands at 5 volts

    Q2 changes its mind at one specific condition: its base-emitter voltage crossing about 0.62 V. Its base is node Z and its emitter is the output, so the condition is

    V(Z) − V(out) = VBE(on)
    V(out) = VZ − VBE(on) ≈ 5.6 V − 0.62 V ≈ 4.98 V
    Q1 turns off when the output rises past this point (with Z clamped at VZ), and stays off at least until it falls back below it.

    This is the same arrangement as an emitter follower, the most basic transistor circuit: an emitter sits one VBE below the base. Here the follower is not supplying the load current itself. It is used purely as a sensor: whenever the emitter (output) tries to sit more than one VBE below the base, the transistor conducts and, via Q1, does something about it.

    Two consequences follow directly.

    • To change the output voltage, change the zener. A 3.9 V zener gives about 3.3 V out; a 12 V zener gives about 11.4 V out (with a supply comfortably above that). There is no divider to adjust. See the questions on modifying it for how to make it adjustable.
    • The output drifts with temperature the way VBE does. A silicon junction's VBE falls by about 2 mV for every degree Celsius it warms up. As Q2 warms, the output rises by about 2 mV/°C. Zeners around 5–6 V have a temperature coefficient close to zero, so the junction dominates. Over a 40 °C swing that is roughly 80 mV, or 1.6 %.

    The average output is slightly below 4.98 V, because the output only touches the threshold at the top of each ripple cycle and spends the rest of the cycle a few millivolts below it. The simulator below shows 4.973 V average with the representative parts.

    Part I · 5

    C2: the part that makes it switch

    Take C2 out and think about what is left. Q2 is a transistor with a gain of a few hundred, comparing the output to a reference. Near the threshold, a millivolt change in the output changes Q2's current only a little. Q2 then drives Q1 only partly on. The circuit would settle into a state where Q1 is half-on, dropping 9 V across itself while passing the load current, which is a linear regulator, and a hot one: 9 V × 0.25 A = 2.25 W in a TO-92 package rated for about 0.6 W. In practice the inductor and output capacitor add enough delay that the loop would probably oscillate anyway, but slowly and with soft, lossy edges.

    C2 turns this sluggish analog loop into a clean digital one. It does three separate things:

    1. Positive feedback on every edge. Any movement at A is copied, scaled by C2/(C1+C2), to Q2's base, in the same direction that Q2 was already heading. A small start toward turning off becomes a full turn-off; a small start toward turning on becomes a full turn-on. In op-amp language, C2 turns Q2 into a Schmitt trigger, but one whose hysteresis exists only briefly after each edge, rather than permanently.
    2. A timed off period. After each turn-off, Z is knocked down by about 1.3 V and must be recharged by RZ before Q2 can turn on again. This forces a minimum off-time and keeps the frequency from running away.
    3. A reverse bias on Q2 during the off period, which makes it immune to noise and ripple on the output while Q1 is off.

    How far Z drops

    ΔVZ = ΔVA × C2C1 + C2
    ΔVZ = 14.15 V × 4.7 nF51.7 nF ≈ 1.29 V
    ΔVA is node A's full swing: from Vin − VCE(sat) ≈ 13.8 V down to −VF of D1 ≈ −0.35 V. This is a capacitive divider: charge leaving C2's left plate has to come from C1 (and to a far smaller extent from RZ and Q2's base capacitance).

    How long Z takes to recover: the off-time

    During the off period RZ drives a current of roughly (Vin − VZ)/RZ into the combined capacitance C1 + C2. Because Vin is so much higher than VZ, that current hardly changes as Z climbs, so the ramp is close to a straight line. The time to climb back up by ΔVZ is charge divided by current:

    toff ≈ (C1 + C2) · ΔVZIRZ = (C1 + C2) · ΔVA · C2 / (C1 + C2)IRZ = C2 · ΔVAIRZ
    toff ≈ 4.7 nF × 14.15 V9.05 mA ≈ 7.35 µs
    IRZ taken at the middle of the ramp: (14 V − 4.95 V) / 1 kΩ ≈ 9.05 mA.

    A result worth stopping on

    C1 cancels out of the off-time. A bigger C1 makes the kick smaller, but it also makes the recovery proportionally slower, and the two effects cancel exactly. The off-time is set by C2, the supply voltage and RZ alone: it is the time RZ takes to replace the charge C2 removed. C1 decides only how deep the kick goes, which matters for noise immunity and for protecting Q2 (see what happens if C1 is too small).

    Once you have a constant off-time, the rest follows from the inductor. In steady state the inductor current must end each cycle where it started, so the current gained during the on-time must equal the current lost during the off-time (this is called volt-second balance):

    (Vin − Vsat − Vout) · ton = (Vout + VF) · toff
    ton = toff · Vout + VFVin − Vsat − Vout = 7.35 µs × 5.338.82 ≈ 4.45 µs

    So the circuit's control law is: stay off for a fixed time set by C2 and RZ; then stay on until the output reaches the threshold. This family of controllers is called constant off-time (COT) or, more loosely, hysteretic or ripple regulation. Commercial controller ICs use the same idea with a precision comparator and a timer. Here, one transistor and one capacitor do both jobs.

    Read down the table and three facts about this circuit emerge.

    • The frequency barely depends on the load. Nothing in the off-time formula involves load current, and the duty cycle only shifts a little with load (through resistive drops). Between 100 mA and 500 mA the simulated frequency stays within 2 %. It does depend on the input voltage: at 9 V in the duty cycle is near 0.6 and the frequency drops to about 38 kHz; at 24 V in it rises to about 130 kHz.
    • The output ripple is mostly CLOAD's ESR. The capacitor's equivalent series resistance (ESR) turns the inductor's triangular ripple current directly into a triangular ripple voltage. The capacitance itself contributes far less. This matters more than usual here, because the regulator decides when to switch by watching that ripple. See why ESR is part of the control loop.
    • The input current is less than half the output current. 14 V × 0.108 A ≈ 1.51 W in, 5 V × 0.25 A ≈ 1.24 W out. A linear regulator would draw the full 0.25 A from the 14 V supply (3.5 W in) and turn 2.25 W of it into heat.

    What the simulator models, and what it leaves out

    It integrates the inductor current, the output capacitor voltage and the voltage on node Z in 2–10 ns steps. Q2 and the zener are treated as a comparator: Q2 is "on" whenever V(Z) − V(out) exceeds 0.62 V. Q1 switches after a delay (0.1 µs to turn on, 0.4 µs storage time to turn off), has a small saturation voltage, and cannot carry more current than its base drive times a gain of 80. C2's coupling is applied as charge sharing with C1. The zener is an ideal clamp; Q2's base is clamped 0.8 V above the output when on and cannot go further than 5.5 V below it (emitter-base breakdown). D1 is a Schottky with about 0.3 V drop. The inductor has 0.3 Ω of resistance; the output capacitor has the ESR you set.

    Left out: the gradual, analog part of each transition (so switching losses are not counted, and the "no C2" case looks cleaner than reality, since a real C2-less circuit would linger half-on), parasitic ringing at node A in discontinuous mode, the zener's soft knee, temperature, and any variation in transistor gain with current. Treat its numbers as a careful sketch, good to perhaps 10–20 %, not as a substitute for SPICE or a breadboard.

    Reading the drawings

    What do the coloured lines on the original drawings mean?

    They are not wires. They are hand-drawn arrows showing where current is flowing in that phase, laid on top of the black-and-red schematic. Each drawing has a key in the top right: thick magenta for the "major" (load) current, thinner colours for the small control currents. The arrowheads show the direction of conventional current (see the next question).

    On this page's redrawn schematic, the same paths appear as translucent bands with moving dots when you choose a phase, using one colour per kind of current that stays the same in every phase.

    Which way does current flow: plus to minus, or minus to plus?

    Both answers are correct, describing different things. Conventional current flows from positive to negative. It is the convention used in every schematic, every formula and every transistor symbol (the arrow on a transistor's emitter points in the conventional-current direction). Electron flow goes the other way, because electrons are negatively charged.

    The drawings and this page use conventional current throughout. Never mix the two in one analysis.

    What do the dots on the wires mean, and why do some wires cross without one?

    A dot marks a junction: every wire touching it is connected. Two wires that cross without a dot are not connected; they just pass over each other on paper. In the original drawing, the line from Q2's emitter to the output crosses the vertical wire through node A without a dot, so those are separate.

    On this page's redrawn schematic, that crossing is drawn with a small hop (a semicircle) to make it unmistakable.

    What are Z, A and B?

    Node names the original author added so the text could refer to them. Z is the zener/reference node (also Q2's base). A is the switch node, where Q1, D1, L1 and C2 meet. B is the regulated output. See the five nodes worth naming.

    What does "Gnd" mean, and is it the same as negative?

    Ground is simply the node that every other voltage is measured from, and it is called 0 V by definition. Here it is the negative terminal of the 14 V supply and also the negative side of the output. When this page says "node A is at −0.35 V", it means A is 0.35 V below ground. Ground is not a special physical thing; it is a reference you choose.

    Why are both ends of the ground line labelled Gnd separately?

    They are the same node. The drawing labels the input ground on the left and output ground on the right so you can see the converter has a common ground: the output's negative is the input's negative. A buck converter cannot provide isolation between input and output.

    What do the transistor symbols tell me?

    The bar is the base. The two angled lines are the collector and the emitter; the one with the arrow is the emitter. The arrow points the way conventional current flows through the base-emitter junction when the transistor is on.

    • Arrow pointing out (Q2, BC337): NPN. Turn it on by making the base about 0.6 V more positive than the emitter. Current flows in at the collector, out at the emitter.
    • Arrow pointing in (Q1, BC327): PNP. Turn it on by making the base about 0.6 V more negative than the emitter. Current flows in at the emitter, out at the collector.

    A mnemonic: NPN = "Not Pointing iN".

    What are the triangle-and-bar symbols?

    Diodes. Conventional current flows easily in the direction the triangle points, and is blocked the other way. The bar is the cathode. D1's triangle points up, from ground toward node A: it conducts when A falls below ground. The zener's symbol has small hooks on the bar, marking it as a diode meant to be operated in reverse breakdown (see what a zener is).

    What is the "+" on CLOAD?

    CLOAD is an electrolytic capacitor, which is polarised: one lead must always be more positive than the other. The "+" marks the lead that goes to the output. Fitted backwards, an electrolytic leaks, heats, and can vent or burst. On the physical part the negative lead is usually the one marked, with a stripe of minus signs, and is the shorter lead.

    Are the resistors drawn as boxes the same as the zigzag ones I've seen elsewhere?

    Yes. The box is the European/IEC symbol and the zigzag is the American/ANSI one. They mean the same component.

    Why does "1" and "2" appear on the inductor?

    Pin numbers, from the drawing program. An ordinary inductor works the same either way round. (In very sensitive designs the "start" of the winding is put on the noisy node A so the outer turns, which are at the quiet output, shield the noisy inner ones, but this circuit doesn't need that level of care.)

    Why is there no load drawn on the original?

    The load is whatever you connect to "Regulated +5v out": a microcontroller, LEDs, a radio. The drawings show the major current arrows heading off the right edge toward it. This page's version draws a dashed resistor labelled "load" so the current path has somewhere to close.

    Concepts from zero

    If you already know what an inductor and a saturated transistor are, skip to the next group.

    What is a voltage regulator for?

    Most electronics needs a fixed supply voltage (5 V, 3.3 V) that stays put whatever the input does and whatever current the circuit draws. Batteries sag as they discharge, car electrical systems swing between 11 and 15 V and spike higher, and wall adaptors vary with load. A regulator takes that messy input and produces a steady output.

    What is the difference between a linear regulator and a switching regulator?

    A linear regulator (78L05, LM317, a zener and a transistor) is a variable resistor in series with the load. To get 5 V from 14 V, it drops the extra 9 V across itself. The current in equals the current out, so the excess power, 9 V × Iload, becomes heat. Efficiency is at best Vout/Vin = 36 % here.

    A switching regulator uses a switch that is either fully on (almost no voltage across it) or fully off (almost no current through it). Either way, very little power is lost in the switch. An inductor and capacitor smooth the chopped waveform back into DC. Because energy is transferred rather than burned, the input current can be much less than the output current, and efficiency of 80–95 % is normal.

    The price is complexity, ripple and electrical noise, and the need for an inductor.

    What does "buck" mean? Are there other kinds?

    A buck converter steps voltage down. The name comes from "bucking" (opposing) the input. Its signature is exactly this arrangement: a switch from the input to an inductor, a diode from ground to the same point, and the inductor's other end going to the output capacitor.

    Siblings: a boost converter steps up (the inductor goes to the input, the switch to ground); a buck-boost or inverting converter can go either way or produce a negative output; flyback and forward converters use a transformer for isolation. All use the same core idea: store energy in a magnetic field, release it where you want it.

    What does an inductor actually do?

    An inductor is a coil of wire, usually around a magnetic core. Current through it builds a magnetic field, and that field stores energy (½·L·I2). The key behaviour: an inductor resists changes in its current. Its defining equation is

    V = L · dIdt
    The voltage across it sets how fast its current changes, not how big the current is.

    Put a steady voltage across an inductor and its current ramps steadily. Try to stop its current suddenly and it produces whatever voltage it takes to keep the current going, which is why node A dives below ground the moment Q1 turns off: the inductor yanks it down until D1 conducts.

    A useful analogy is a heavy flywheel or water in a long pipe: it takes effort to speed up and keeps going when you stop pushing.

    What does a capacitor actually do?

    A capacitor stores charge on two plates separated by an insulator. Its defining equation is the mirror of an inductor's: a capacitor resists changes in its voltage.

    I = C · dVdt
    The current into it sets how fast its voltage changes.

    The consequence used by C2 in this circuit: since the voltage across a capacitor cannot jump, if one plate's voltage jumps, the other plate's voltage jumps by the same amount (unless something else supplies the charge to stop it). That is why a 14 V step at node A appears at node Z, reduced only by C1 sharing the charge.

    What does a diode do?

    It lets current through in one direction (anode to cathode, the way the triangle points) once the forward voltage exceeds a threshold, about 0.6–0.7 V for silicon or 0.2–0.45 V for a Schottky. In the other direction it blocks, up to its reverse voltage rating. In this circuit D1 acts as an automatic switch: it closes by itself whenever Q1 opens and the inductor drags node A negative.

    What is a zener diode?

    A diode designed to break down at a precise reverse voltage, and to survive doing it. Once the reverse voltage reaches its rating (5.6 V here), it conducts as much current as necessary to stop the voltage going any higher, a bit like a pressure-relief valve. Feed it from a resistor and it gives you a fairly stable reference voltage: the resistor (RZ) drops whatever voltage the supply has spare, and the zener holds its own voltage.

    It needs a minimum current to be "in regulation", typically 1–5 mA for a small zener. Below that, its voltage sags. Above its maximum power rating (often 0.5 W or 1.3 W), it overheats.

    How does a bipolar transistor work, in practical terms?

    A small current flowing through the base-emitter junction allows a much larger current to flow through the collector-emitter path. The ratio is the current gain, hFE or β, typically 100–600 for small transistors. The base-emitter junction behaves like a diode, so it needs about 0.6–0.7 V across it to conduct.

    Two regimes matter here:

    • Active (linear) region: collector current = β × base current. The transistor behaves like a current-controlled valve, and can have a large voltage across it while conducting, so it can get hot.
    • Saturation: you supply far more base current than needed for the actual collector current. The transistor is "fully on" with only 0.1–0.3 V across it, like a closed switch.

    A switching regulator wants its switch to spend as close to zero time as possible in the active region.

    What is saturation, and what is storage time?

    Saturation is the state where a bipolar transistor has more base current than its collector current requires, so it can't conduct any harder: its collector-emitter voltage collapses to its minimum (VCE(sat), about 0.1–0.3 V for a BC327 at a few hundred mA). Engineers express the overdrive as a forced beta: actual collector current divided by base current supplied. Here, about 0.3 A / 16 mA ≈ 19, far below the BC327's real gain of 100+, so Q1 is firmly saturated.

    The cost is storage time. A saturated transistor's base region is flooded with excess charge carriers. When you remove the base drive, the transistor keeps conducting until that charge has been swept out, which takes from a hundred nanoseconds to a few microseconds depending on how hard it was driven and how hard you pull the charge out. R1 helps pull it out. That delay is why the turn-off edge in this circuit lags the decision to turn off, and why the output overshoots the threshold a little each cycle.

    What does "self-oscillating" mean?

    Most switching regulators have a clock (an oscillator running at a fixed frequency) and a control circuit that decides how much of each clock cycle the switch spends on. This circuit has no clock. The switching rhythm emerges from the circuit's own behaviour: the comparator, the delay built by C2 and RZ, and the inductor and capacitor. It oscillates because it is unstable on purpose: there is no steady state where Q1 can sit still.

    What is positive feedback, and isn't feedback supposed to be negative?

    Negative feedback opposes change: if the output rises, the circuit acts to bring it down. It makes things stable and accurate, and the overall regulator loop (output too high → switch off → output falls) is negative feedback.

    Positive feedback reinforces change: a small push becomes a big one. It makes a circuit snap decisively between two states instead of settling in the middle. Light switches use mechanical positive feedback (a spring over a cam) so they click. C2 is the electrical equivalent: once Q1 begins to switch, C2 pushes Q2 to make it switch faster and further.

    This regulator uses both: slow negative feedback for regulation, fast positive feedback for clean switching.

    What is hysteresis?

    Having different thresholds depending on which way you are going. A thermostat that turns the heating on at 19 °C and off at 21 °C has 2 °C of hysteresis. Without it, the heating would click on and off rapidly at exactly 20 °C as the temperature jittered.

    In this circuit, the hysteresis is temporary: right after a turn-off, Z is 1.3 V lower, so the turn-on threshold is effectively 1.3 V higher. As RZ recharges Z, that extra threshold shrinks back to zero. A regular Schmitt trigger has permanent hysteresis; this one has "hysteresis that decays", which is why the result is a constant off-time rather than a fixed voltage window.

    What is "duty cycle"?

    The fraction of each cycle the switch spends on. A 38 % duty cycle means Q1 is on for 38 % of the time. For an ideal buck converter, the output is the input multiplied by the duty cycle: 14 V × 0.357 = 5 V. The real figure is a little higher (about 0.38) because the switch and diode drop some voltage.

    Why does averaging a chopped 14 V waveform give 5 V?

    Node A spends about 38 % of its time at +13.8 V and about 62 % at −0.35 V. Its average is 0.38 × 13.8 + 0.62 × (−0.35) ≈ 5.0 V. An inductor cannot have a DC voltage across it in steady state (its current would ramp forever), so the inductor's two ends must have the same average voltage. The output end is held steady by CLOAD, so the output equals node A's average. The LC pair is a low-pass filter that passes the average and blocks the switching.

    What are µH, nF, kHz and the rest?
    • µ (micro) = one millionth. 220 µH = 0.000220 henries. 7 µs = 7 millionths of a second.
    • n (nano) = one billionth. 4.7 nF = 0.0000000047 farads. Also written 4n7.
    • m (milli) = one thousandth. 250 mA = 0.25 A.
    • k (kilo) = one thousand. 1 kΩ = 1000 ohms. 85 kHz = 85,000 cycles per second.
    • p (pico) = one trillionth, for very small capacitors.

    Component markings often put the multiplier where the decimal point would go: 4k7 = 4.7 kΩ, 2n2 = 2.2 nF, 4R7 = 4.7 Ω. Ceramic capacitors use a three-digit code in picofarads: 472 = 47 × 102 pF = 4.7 nF; 473 = 47 nF; 104 = 100 nF.

    Each part's job

    Why is Q1 a PNP transistor rather than an NPN?

    Q1 is a high-side switch: it sits between the positive supply and the load. A PNP's emitter goes to the positive supply, and you turn it on by pulling its base about 0.7 V below the supply, which is easy: just let a current flow out of the base towards ground, which is exactly what Q2 and R2 do.

    An NPN in the same spot would have its emitter at node A, which swings up to nearly 14 V. To turn it fully on you would need its base at about 14.7 V, above the supply. That needs a "bootstrap" circuit or a separate higher supply. The PNP avoids the problem entirely, at the cost of PNPs being slightly worse transistors than NPNs of the same size.

    Why a BC327 and BC337 specifically?

    They are a cheap, very common complementary pair (one PNP, one NPN with matching specs) in the small TO-92 package, rated for 45 V and 800 mA, with good gain at hundreds of milliamps. That 800 mA rating is unusual for a TO-92 part: the more common BC557/BC547 and 2N3906/2N3904 pairs are rated for 100–200 mA, too little for Q1 here.

    Q2 only carries Q1's base current (tens of mA) so almost any small NPN would do. Using a BC337 is mostly convenience. The suffix (-16, -25, -40) marks the gain group; -40 parts have the most gain.

    What exactly does R1 do?

    R1 connects Q1's base to its emitter. When Q2 stops pulling current, R1 pulls Q1's base back up to the emitter voltage, so Q1's base-emitter voltage is zero and it is firmly off. It also gives Q1's stored base charge a path out, shortening the storage time.

    Without R1, Q1's base would float when Q2 turned off. Leakage current through Q1's collector-base junction would then be amplified by Q1's gain, Q1 would partly conduct, and turn-off would be slow and lossy, especially when hot.

    R1 also wastes a little drive: with Q1 on, it carries 0.8 V / 1 kΩ = 0.8 mA that does not go into Q1's base. A smaller R1 turns Q1 off faster but needs more drive from Q2.

    What exactly does R2 do?

    R2 sets how much current Q2 pulls out of Q1's base. With Q1 on, the voltage across R2 is the supply minus Q1's VBE, minus Q2's small collector-emitter drop, minus the output (because Q2's emitter is on the output): 14 − 0.8 − 0.1 − 5 ≈ 8.1 V. Through 470 Ω that is about 17 mA. Subtract R1's 0.8 mA and Q1 gets about 16 mA of base current.

    Too large an R2 and Q1 is underdriven: it leaves saturation at high currents and runs hot. Too small and you waste drive current, increase Q1's storage time, and allow bigger start-up and fault currents.

    Without R2, Q2 would connect Q1's base almost directly to the output, and the current would be limited only by the two transistors' gains: a reliable way to destroy one of them.

    Why does Q2's emitter go to the output rather than to ground?

    That connection is the voltage sensing. Q2 turns on when its base is about 0.62 V above its emitter. With the base held at the zener voltage and the emitter on the output, Q2 conducts exactly when the output is below VZ − 0.62 V. No divider, no separate comparator, no op-amp: the transistor's own base-emitter junction does the comparison.

    A useful side effect: Q1's base drive current (about 17 mA during the on-time) flows through Q2 into the output, not into ground. It is delivered to the load, so it isn't entirely wasted. You can see this in the original phase 1 drawing, where the "base current path" ends at the output.

    What does RZ do? It looks like a plain bias resistor.

    It has three jobs at once:

    1. Bias the zener: supply enough current that the zener is in regulation. At 14 V that's (14 − 5.6)/1 kΩ = 8.4 mA.
    2. Supply Q2's base current while Q2 is on (well under 1 mA).
    3. Set the off-time: after each turn-off, RZ is the only thing recharging C1 and C2. Its current sets how long the off period lasts: toff ≈ C2 · ΔVA / IRZ.

    That third role is easy to miss, and it means changing RZ changes the switching frequency.

    What does C1 do?

    C1 sits directly across the zener. Its jobs:

    • Limit C2's kick. C1 and C2 form a capacitive divider, so C1 decides how much of node A's 14 V swing reaches Q2's base. With C1 ten times C2, about 1/11 of it does (1.3 V).
    • Steady the reference. Zeners are electrically noisy, especially near their knee. C1 filters that noise.
    • Provide charge. When C2's kick arrives, C1 supplies the charge (phase 2's "C1 discharges into C2") so that node Z moves by a controlled amount.
    What happens if C1 is too small, or missing?

    Then nearly the entire 14 V swing of node A reaches Q2's base. On turn-off, Z would be dragged to around −8 V while Q2's emitter is at +5 V: 13 V of reverse bias across a junction rated for 5 V (VEBO for the BC337). The junction breaks down (it acts like a zener), which limits the damage, but repeated emitter-base breakdown slowly degrades a transistor's gain. The simulator flags this case ("Tiny C1" preset).

    A rule of thumb from the arithmetic: keep (Vin + VF) · C2/(C1 + C2) below about VZ − Vout + 5 V ≈ 5.6 V. At 14 V in, that means C1 > 1.6 × C2. A factor of 10 gives plenty of margin, and also gives margin if you later raise the input voltage.

    Note that C1 does not affect the off-time (see the derivation), so you can change it freely for these reasons without retuning the frequency.

    What does C2 do, in one paragraph?

    It couples the big voltage swing at node A back into Q2's base. That gives positive feedback (clean, fast switching), a temporary reverse bias on Q2 after each turn-off (noise immunity), and a timed off period while RZ recharges it (setting the frequency). See the full section.

    What would happen with no C2 at all?

    The circuit would lose its positive feedback and its timer. Q2 would act as a plain high-gain amplifier driving Q1, and Q1 would spend much more time in its active region. Depending on exact parts and layout, you would get one of: slow, irregular oscillation with soft edges and hot transistors; oscillation at a high, uncontrolled frequency set by stray delays; or Q1 sitting half-on as a linear regulator, overheating. None of those is good. C2 is the component that turns the circuit from "an amplifier with an inductor on it" into a switching regulator.

    The simulator's "Tiny C2" preset shows the frequency soaring to hundreds of kHz, but its comparator is an ideal switch, so it cannot show the half-on heating that a real circuit without C2 would suffer.

    Why is D1 needed? Couldn't the inductor just stop?

    Current in an inductor cannot stop instantly. If Q1 turned off with nowhere for the current to go, the inductor would produce whatever voltage it takes to keep it flowing: hundreds of volts, negative, at node A, which would break down Q1 in the first cycle. D1 gives the current a path: from ground, up through D1, through L1, into the load. It is called the freewheel, flywheel or catch diode.

    D1 is also where the energy stored during the on-time is delivered to the load during the off-time. It isn't just protection; it is half of the power path.

    Why should D1 be a Schottky diode? Would a 1N4001 work?

    Two reasons, and the second is the important one.

    1. Forward voltage. D1 carries the full load current for 60–65 % of every cycle. A Schottky drops 0.3–0.45 V; a silicon rectifier drops 0.8–1 V. At 250 mA, that is the difference between about 50 mW and 140 mW lost.
    2. Reverse recovery. A normal silicon diode like the 1N4001 stores charge while conducting and takes several microseconds to stop conducting when reversed. When Q1 turns on, that diode is briefly a short circuit from node A to ground: Q1 dumps a large current spike straight through it. That wastes power, stresses Q1, and creates noise. A Schottky has essentially no stored charge, so it switches off instantly.

    A 1N4001 might "work" at very light loads, but it is the wrong part. If you have no Schottky, a fast-recovery diode (UF4001, 1N4148 for very small currents) is a better substitute than a 1N400x.

    Why is the output capacitor called CLOAD?

    The original author's name for it: the capacitor that sits across the load. Elsewhere you would see it called Cout or the output filter capacitor. It averages the inductor's triangular current into a steady output voltage, and supplies sudden load demands until the regulator can respond.

    What is ESR and why does it matter so much here?

    Equivalent Series Resistance is the small resistance in series with every real capacitor, from its leads, plates and electrolyte. A general-purpose aluminium electrolytic might have 0.1–1 Ω; a low-ESR type 0.02–0.1 Ω; a ceramic a few milliohms.

    The inductor's ripple current flows through it, so the ESR creates a ripple voltage in step with the inductor current: ΔV = ΔIL × ESR. In most regulators that is only a nuisance. In this one it is part of the control loop, because Q2 decides when to turn off by watching the output voltage. A clean triangle of ripple, in phase with the inductor current, gives Q2 a clear, timely signal.

    • Too much ESR (1 Ω or more): large ripple, roughly 150 mV p-p in the simulator's "High-ESR cap" preset, and a lower average output.
    • Too little ESR (all-ceramic output): the ripple becomes tiny and is the integral of the inductor current, so it lags. The on-time can extend, the inductor current grows, and the frequency becomes irregular. The off-time timer helps, but you lose the clean ripple-terminated on-time. The cure is a few tenths of an ohm in series with a ceramic, or simply using an ordinary electrolytic.
    Why is the zener 5.6 V when the output is 5 V?

    Because the output settles one base-emitter drop below the zener voltage: 5.6 V − 0.62 V ≈ 4.98 V. See why the output lands at 5 V.

    Does the zener current change during the cycle?

    Yes, a lot, and the original drawings show it. In phases 1 and 4 (Z clamped at 5.6 V) the zener carries most of RZ's current. In phases 2 and 3 (Z knocked below 5.6 V) the zener carries nothing at all: all of RZ's current goes into recharging C1 and C2. That's why the phase 2 and 3 drawings have no zener current arrow. During phase 4 the zener briefly carries an extra surge, absorbing C2's upward kick.

    The switching cycle

    Why does node A go below ground?

    When Q1 turns off, the inductor is still carrying current toward the output. Nothing is supplying it from the 14 V side any more, so the inductor pulls node A down, as far as it takes to find a path. The first path it finds is D1, which starts conducting when its anode (ground) is about 0.35 V above its cathode (A). So A stops at about −0.35 V. With a silicon diode it would be about −0.8 V.

    The original says "C1 discharges into C2" during turn-off. What does that mean physically?

    Just before turn-off, C1 has 5.6 V across it, and C2 has (13.8 − 5.6) = 8.2 V across it (Z side negative relative to A). Node A then falls by 14.15 V. For C2's voltage not to change instantly, Z would have to fall by 14.15 V too, but Z is also attached to C1, which resists. The two capacitors share charge until they agree: charge flows out of C1, through node Z, into C2's left plate. C1's voltage drops by 1.3 V; C2's voltage changes by the remaining 12.85 V. That flow is the blue arrow in the phase 2 drawing.

    And "RZ current charges C2" in the same phase?

    RZ is always feeding current into node Z. Once Z drops below the zener voltage, the zener stops conducting, and RZ's current has nowhere to go except into the capacitors on node Z. Part of it goes into C1 and part through C2 toward node A. Both arrows in phases 2 and 3 are this same recharge, which continues until Z is back at 5.6 V.

    Why is phase 3 called the "delay period"?

    Because Q2 is locked off for a set time regardless of the output. Q2's base starts about 0.7 V below its emitter and must climb to 0.62 V above it before Q2 can turn on. The output's own fall contributes only tens of millivolts of that 1.3 V; RZ recharging Z provides the rest. So the off period is effectively a timer, and phase 3 is that timer running.

    Why does the major current keep flowing to the load while Q1 is off?

    Energy stored in the inductor's magnetic field during the on-time. During the off-time the inductor acts as a source, pushing current out into the load and pulling it up from ground through D1. Its current gradually falls as that energy is delivered. Before it has fallen very far, Q1 turns on again and tops it up. The current never stops (at normal loads); it just swings up and down in a triangle around the load current.

    Which is faster, turning on or turning off? Why?

    Turn-on. Before Q1 can turn off, the excess charge in its saturated base must be removed (storage time), typically a few hundred nanoseconds with these parts. Turn-on has no equivalent delay: as soon as base current flows, collector current starts. The asymmetry is built into the simulator (0.1 µs on, 0.4 µs off).

    Why does the output overshoot the threshold a little every cycle?

    Two delays. First, Q1's storage time keeps it on for a few hundred nanoseconds after Q2 decides to turn it off, during which the inductor current keeps climbing. Second, even after Q1 turns off, the inductor current is still above the load current, so the output capacitor keeps charging until the inductor current has fallen back to the load current. The output peaks partway into the off period. This overshoot is part of the ripple and is perfectly normal.

    Can the circuit get stuck on or stuck off?

    Stuck on happens when the output can't reach the threshold: input voltage too low (dropout), load too heavy (Q1 out of drive), or output shorted. This is the dangerous case, since Q1 then carries everything and nothing limits it.

    Stuck off is unlikely in normal operation: RZ always recharges Z eventually. It could happen if the output were held above the threshold by something external (another supply back-feeding it), which is harmless, or if RZ were open circuit or the zener shorted.

    What happens at power-on?

    Everything starts at zero. As the supply rises, RZ charges C1 and C2 and Z rises. As soon as Z reaches about 0.6 V, Q2 conducts (its emitter is on the output, still at 0 V), Q1 turns on, and A jumps up, kicking Z up further. Q2's base-emitter junction clamps Z about 0.8 V above the output, and the zener clamps it at 5.6 V.

    Q1 now stays on continuously while the output is below the threshold. The inductor current ramps up and keeps ramping, because the output is low and the voltage across the inductor is large. It is eventually limited only by Q1's gain times its base drive. With the representative parts the simulator shows about 2 A for a few hundred microseconds. When the output finally reaches 4.98 V, normal switching takes over, with a small overshoot as the large inductor current dies away. Reducing this inrush is one of the most useful improvements; see soft start.

    What is the difference between continuous and discontinuous conduction?

    In continuous conduction mode (CCM), the inductor current never reaches zero: it swings, for example, between 0.15 and 0.33 A. That is the normal case at moderate loads.

    When the load current is less than half the ripple current (below about 90 mA here), the inductor current reaches zero before the off period ends. D1 stops conducting, and node A, no longer connected to anything firmly, rings around the output voltage with the inductor and stray capacitances. That is discontinuous conduction mode (DCM). Regulation still works; the waveforms just look messier. In this circuit the jump of node A from −0.35 V up toward 5 V as D1 stops conducting also couples a small upward kick into Z through C2, which shortens the off-time, so the frequency rises at light load.

    Does it still work with no load at all?

    Yes. Q1's base drive current, which flows through Q2 into the output, acts as a small load (a few milliamps averaged), so the circuit still switches, delivering short pulses. The output may creep up a little because there is nothing to pull it down between pulses except CLOAD's leakage and that drive current. A small resistor across the output (a few kΩ) keeps things tidy, and many designs include one as a minimum load.

    How does it react if the load suddenly increases?

    Quickly. The extra current initially comes out of CLOAD, so the output dips. At the next decision point Q2 sees the lower output, and the following on-time is extended (Q1 stays on until the output recovers), so the inductor current ramps up to meet the new demand within a cycle or two. Hysteretic regulators are known for very fast load response, because there is no error amplifier with a slow compensation network to wait for: each cycle reacts directly to the output voltage.

    How does it react if the input voltage changes?

    The on-time shortens automatically when the input rises (the inductor current climbs faster, so the threshold is reached sooner), and the off-time shortens slightly too (RZ's current rises with the input). The average output barely moves; the simulator shows under 20 mV of change from 9 to 24 V in. The frequency, though, roughly triples over the same range.

    Numbers and performance

    What switching frequency does it run at?

    It depends on your parts. With the representative values, about 85 kHz at 14 V in. The relationship is f ≈ (1 − D) / toff with toff ≈ C2 · ΔVA / IRZ. Halve C2 and the frequency roughly doubles.

    Keep the frequency where the BC327 can switch cleanly. Since the turn-off delay is a few hundred nanoseconds, 20–100 kHz is a comfortable range. At several hundred kHz, Q1's transitions become a significant fraction of each cycle and switching losses rise.

    Why isn't the frequency constant, and is that a problem?

    Because there's no clock, only a timer for the off period. The on-time is whatever it takes to restore the output. For most uses a changing frequency is fine. It matters for radio receivers and audio equipment (a switching frequency or its harmonics can land on a sensitive frequency), and for meeting EMC regulations, where a fixed frequency is easier to filter and certify.

    How accurate is the output voltage?

    It is set by a zener (typically ±5 % tolerance, ±2 % for the better grades) minus a VBE (which varies about ±50 mV between parts and drifts −2 mV/°C). Expect the output to be within roughly ±5 % of nominal from one build to the next. Once built, it is stable: load and line changes move it by only tens of millivolts. If you need better initial accuracy, use a TL431 reference or trim with a pot.

    What does load regulation and line regulation mean, and how good are they?

    Load regulation: how much the output changes as the load current changes. Line regulation: how much it changes as the input voltage changes. Both are good here, because the output is always being driven to the same threshold. In the simulator, the output moves by under 10 mV from 100 to 500 mA, and under 20 mV from 9 to 24 V in. A real build will be somewhat worse, from the zener's dynamic resistance (its voltage rises slightly with current, and RZ's current rises with the input) and from Q2's VBE changing slightly with its collector current.

    How much ripple is on the output?

    With the representative parts, about 18 mV peak-to-peak, nearly all from CLOAD's ESR. The formula is ΔV ≈ ΔIL × ESR + ΔIL / (8 · f · C). On top of that you will see narrow spikes at each switching edge, caused by fast current changes in the wiring; these are usually much bigger on a scope than they really are, see measuring with a scope.

    How efficient is it?

    Around 80 % at 250 mA with the representative parts, rising toward the mid 80s at heavier loads and falling sharply at light loads. At 10 mA it is around 25 %, because RZ and the zener waste about 120 mW regardless of load. A dedicated buck controller IC would typically reach 85–92 %. Compared with a linear regulator at 14 V to 5 V (36 % at best), it is a huge improvement at any useful load.

    Why is the input current smaller than the output current?

    Energy is conserved. Power in = power out + losses. If 1.24 W goes out at 5 V, and the losses are about 0.27 W, then 1.51 W must come in at 14 V: 1.51 / 14 = 0.108 A, against 0.25 A out. The inductor acts as a sort of "DC transformer": it converts high voltage and low current into low voltage and high current. The extra output current comes from D1 during the off-time, which is current that never passed through the supply.

    How hot do the parts get?

    At 250 mA: Q1 dissipates roughly 20 mW conduction plus 20–60 mW switching (warm at most). D1, about 50 mW (barely warm). RZ, about 75 mW (a 0.25 W resistor is fine). The zener, about 50 mW. Nothing needs a heatsink. At 500–700 mA, Q1 gets noticeably warm (a TO-92 can dissipate about 0.6 W in free air at 25 °C). If Q1 is hot to touch at moderate load, it is probably not saturating; check R2.

    What is the maximum output current?

    Practically, about 500–600 mA with a BC327 as Q1, so that the peak inductor current (load plus half the ripple) stays below its 800 mA rating with margin, and Q1 stays saturated with the base drive R2 provides. The inductor must also be rated for that peak current without saturating (see choosing an inductor). For more current, see getting more current.

    Building and testing

    What kind of inductor should I use?

    A power inductor: a ferrite drum ("dog-bone") or toroid with a few dozen turns of fairly thick wire. Check three numbers on the datasheet:

    • Inductance: 100–470 µH suits this circuit at tens of kHz. More inductance means less ripple current.
    • Saturation current (Isat): must exceed your peak inductor current, with margin. Above it, the core saturates, inductance falls off a cliff and current spikes. Keep in mind the start-up peak is higher than the running peak.
    • DC resistance (DCR): the lower the better; 0.1–0.5 Ω is typical at this size.

    Avoid the tiny resistor-shaped "RF chokes" in the same values: their saturation current is often only tens of milliamps. Salvaged inductors from old PC power supplies and motherboards are often ideal.

    What kind of capacitors should I use for C1 and C2?

    Small ceramic (X7R) or film (polyester/MKT) types. C2 sees the full input voltage swing, so it needs a voltage rating above your maximum input (50 V is a convenient value). C1 only sees about 6 V. Avoid Y5V/Z5U ceramics for C2: their capacitance varies dramatically with voltage and temperature, and since C2 sets the off-time, the frequency would wander. NP0/C0G or film are the most stable.

    Can I build it on a breadboard?

    Yes, at these frequencies and currents it will work, and it is how many people first see it running. Expect more switching noise and ringing than on a PCB, because breadboard strips add inductance and capacitance. Keep the "hot loop" short: the path from the input supply's decoupling capacitor, through Q1, through D1, back to ground. That loop carries sharp-edged current and is the main source of noise. Twist or keep close the supply leads, and put a 10–100 µF electrolytic plus a 100 nF ceramic across the 14 V input right at the circuit.

    Do I need an input capacitor? There's none on the drawing.

    You should add one. Q1 draws current from the supply in sharp-edged pulses: about 0.3 A for 38 % of the time and nothing for the rest. The input wiring has inductance, so those pulses create voltage spikes and drops at the circuit's supply pins, and broadcast noise back along the supply leads. A capacitor right at the circuit (an electrolytic of tens of µF plus a 100 nF ceramic) supplies the pulses locally, so the supply leads carry only the smooth average. The drawings are diagrams of the principle and leave it out for clarity.

    How should I measure it with an oscilloscope?
    • Node A (the square wave) is the best first look: it shows frequency, duty cycle, and the cleanliness of the edges. Use DC coupling, 5 V/div.
    • Node Z shows the sawtooth timer (it should resemble the original cro.jpg). Use AC coupling or a 1 V/div DC setting with the trace offset.
    • Output ripple: AC coupling, 10–20 mV/div, and the bandwidth limit switched on.
    • Inductor current: put a 0.1 Ω resistor in series with the inductor's output end and measure across it (1 mV per 10 mA), or use a current probe.

    Use the short spring ground clip on the probe tip, not the long ground lead with the crocodile clip. The long lead forms a loop antenna that picks up the switching fields and shows enormous fake spikes on the ripple, which is the most common measurement mistake on switching supplies.

    How does the dummy load in the photo work, and how do I make one?

    The photo shows a TO-220 power transistor, a potentiometer and a big wirewound resistor. The transistor acts as an adjustable current sink: the pot sets its base current (or base voltage), which sets how much current flows through it, and the power resistor in series takes a share of the heat so the transistor doesn't have to dissipate it all. Turning the pot sweeps the load from nothing to the full rated current, which is invaluable for seeing how the regulator responds to load changes.

    A simpler and more stable version uses a power MOSFET or an NPN with a small emitter resistor (for example 1 Ω), and a pot from a fixed voltage driving the base or gate: the current is then roughly (pot voltage − VBE) / Remitter, independent of the supply voltage. Mount the transistor on a heatsink; at 5 V and 0.5 A it dissipates up to 2.5 W.

    The simplest option of all is just a power resistor: 10 Ω draws 0.5 A at 5 V and dissipates 2.5 W, so it needs a 5 W rating.

    It doesn't oscillate. What should I check?
    1. Is the zener the right way round? Its cathode (the barred end) goes to Z. Backwards, it acts as an ordinary diode and holds Z at 0.7 V.
    2. Are Q1 and Q2 the right way round? TO-92 pinouts differ: the BC327/BC337 is usually C-B-E reading left to right with the flat face toward you, which is the opposite of the 2N3904/2N3906 (E-B-C). Check the datasheet for your exact part.
    3. Is C2 actually connected between Z and A? Without it, the circuit doesn't snap; see what happens without C2.
    4. Is the output stuck at the input voltage? Q1 is shorted or stuck on, or Q2 is wired so it's always on.
    5. Is the output at 0 V? Q1 not turning on: check R2, Q2, and that RZ is feeding the zener.
    6. Is the output at about 5 V but there's no switching on node A? The load may be too light (it switches in occasional bursts) or Q1 may be acting as a linear regulator; touch it to see if it's hot.
    Q1 is getting hot. Why?

    The likely reasons, roughly in order: (1) Q1 isn't saturating because it isn't getting enough base current: check R2 and that Q2 is fully on; (2) D1 is a slow silicon diode causing a current spike every turn-on, see Schottky; (3) the switching edges are slow because C2 is missing, too small or open; (4) the load is too heavy or the output is shorted; (5) the inductor is saturating, causing large current peaks.

    The switching frequency is wildly different from the calculation. Why?

    Check C2's actual value (ceramic capacitors can be 20–80 % below their marking at voltage, especially Y5V types) and RZ. Also check whether the circuit is in discontinuous mode (light load), where the frequency rises, or whether the output capacitor has very low ESR, which can make the on-time irregular. Finally, check you are measuring the fundamental, not a burst repetition rate: at light loads the circuit can produce groups of pulses with gaps between them.

    Is a 14 V supply safe to work with?

    Yes, 14 V can't give you a dangerous shock. The hazards are burns from overheated parts (a shorted output can make Q1 or wiring very hot quickly), electrolytic capacitors fitted backwards (they can vent), and the power source itself: a car battery can deliver hundreds of amps into a short, enough to melt wires. Always put a fuse in series with a battery supply. Never adapt this circuit to run from mains voltage.

    Changing it

    How do I get a different output voltage?

    Change the zener: Vout ≈ VZ − 0.62 V. For 3.3 V use a 3.9 V zener; for 9 V use a 9.1 or 10 V zener; for 12 V use 12 or 13 V. Keep the input at least about 3 V above the output so Q1 can saturate and RZ can supply the zener. Recheck RZ's current: (Vin − VZ)/RZ should stay above a few mA. Low-voltage zeners (below about 4.7 V) have soft knees and are less precise, so a TL431 is better there.

    Can I make the output adjustable?

    Yes. Several ways, from simplest:

    • Put a few diodes in series with the zener (each adds about 0.6 V) and switch them in and out: coarse steps.
    • Feed Q2's emitter from a divider on the output rather than the output itself, so Q2 sees a fraction of the output. The output then becomes (VZ − VBE) × (divider ratio). Q1's base drive current then flows through the divider, so keep the divider low in resistance, or buffer it.
    • Replace the zener with a TL431 adjustable reference and a pot.
    Can I use a TL431 instead of the zener?

    Yes, and it's a good upgrade. A TL431 is an IC "programmable zener" with a 2.5 V internal reference and much better accuracy (±0.5–2 %) and temperature stability than a zener. Wired with two resistors, it acts like a zener whose voltage you set: V = 2.5 V × (1 + Rtop/Rbottom). Use a pot for one resistor and the output is adjustable. It needs at least 1 mA of current, which RZ already supplies. Keep C1 across it, but check the TL431 datasheet's stability chart: some values of capacitance directly across a TL431 can make it oscillate on its own.

    How do I get more output current?
    • Bigger Q1: a BD140 or BD136 (1.5 A), a TIP32 or TIP42 (3–6 A) or similar power PNP. These have lower gain at high current, so reduce R2 to supply more base current (forced beta around 10–20), and accept that Q2 then carries more current too.
    • A P-channel MOSFET for Q1: lower losses, faster switching, no storage time. Its gate must be driven between the supply and about 10 V below it; at 14 V most parts can take the full supply on the gate, but above about 18 V you need a zener to clamp the gate-source voltage. R1 becomes the gate pull-up and R2 the gate drive resistor. Gate capacitance makes turn-off slower with large R1, so values drop to the hundreds of ohms.
    • Bigger D1 (a 3 A Schottky such as the 1N5822) and an inductor rated for the higher peak current.

    Don't skip current limiting at higher currents: the damage from a short grows with the current available.

    How can I add short-circuit or current limiting?

    A classic addition: put a small sense resistor (for example 1 Ω for about 0.6 A) between the +14 V rail and Q1's emitter, and a small PNP transistor with its emitter on the rail and its base at Q1's emitter. When the voltage across the sense resistor exceeds about 0.6 V, the extra PNP turns on and pulls Q1's base toward its emitter, starving Q1 of drive. That gives cycle-by-cycle current limiting. With this circuit's positive feedback, it is better if the limit transistor's collector also pulls Z down (through a resistor), so the limit triggers a proper turn-off and off period rather than a slow linear-mode current limit.

    A simpler fallback is a fuse or a polyfuse (PTC resettable fuse) on the input, which protects the wiring and supply but not necessarily Q1.

    Can I add a soft start?

    Yes. The aim is to make the reference rise slowly at power-on, so the output follows it up rather than Q1 slamming the inductor current to its limit. Making C1 much bigger (for example 10 µF) slows Z's rise: Z charges through RZ with time constant RZ × C1 = 10 ms. As C1 doesn't affect the off-time, this costs nothing in normal operation. The only side effect is that the C2 kick becomes very small (about 1/2000 of the swing), so you lose most of the snap-off reverse bias; C2 still gives positive feedback but with less margin. A compromise is a separate larger capacitor fed through a diode, or simply a few µF for C1.

    Can I make RZ bigger to save power?

    Up to a point. Doubling RZ halves its standing loss (to about 60 mW at 14 V), but:

    • it also halves the recharge current, so the off-time doubles and the frequency halves (unless you also halve C2);
    • the zener current falls to about 4 mA, closer to its soft knee, making the reference less precise;
    • at low input voltages RZ may no longer supply enough current to the zener at all.

    If you increase RZ, reduce C2 by the same factor to keep the frequency. Watch that C1 is still at least ten times C2.

    Can I run it from 24 V, or 9 V?

    24 V: yes, with care. RZ dissipates (24 − 5.6)2/1 kΩ = 0.34 W, so use a 0.5 W resistor or raise RZ (then shrink C2). C2 must be rated above 24 V. The duty cycle drops to about 22 % and the frequency rises to about 130 kHz. The C2 kick grows to 2.2 V, still safely below Q2's emitter-base limit.

    9 V: yes. The duty cycle rises to about 60 % and the frequency falls to about 38 kHz. Efficiency improves (less voltage to drop). RZ's current falls to about 3.4 mA, which is adequate. Below about 8 V you approach dropout.

    Try both with the simulator's "24 V in" and "9 V in" presets.

    Can I use it as a constant-current LED driver?

    Yes, with a change to what Q2 senses. Put the LEDs in place of the load, with a small sense resistor in series from the LEDs to ground. Instead of comparing the output voltage with the zener, compare the sense resistor's voltage with a reference. In the simplest version, Q2's base is driven from a low reference (a couple of diodes) and its emitter goes to the top of the sense resistor, so the regulator holds the sense voltage at about (reference − 0.62 V). Many hobby LED drivers are built exactly this way. A buck regulator is especially well suited to LEDs, because the inductor current is the LED current.

    Can it make a negative voltage, or step up?

    Not as drawn: it is a buck (step-down) converter. The same control idea (a transistor comparator with capacitor feedback from the switch node) can drive a boost or inverting topology, but the power stage must be rearranged and the sensing reworked. For a quick negative supply, a charge pump driven from node A is simpler: two capacitors and two diodes can produce roughly −(Vin − 1.5 V) at small currents.

    Failure and safety

    What happens if the output is short-circuited?

    The output can never reach the threshold, so Q2 and Q1 stay on continuously. The inductor current ramps up until Q1 runs out of base drive, at roughly hFE × 28 mA, which could be 1.5–7 A depending on the individual transistor. Q1 then sits in its active region with nearly 14 V across it and that current through it. It will overheat within milliseconds to seconds. Expect a dead BC327. Fit a fuse, and see current limiting.

    What happens if Q1 fails short?

    Node A is then permanently connected to the 14 V input, and the output rises to 14 V (through the inductor). Anything on the 5 V output that can't tolerate 14 V will be damaged. This is the main hazard of any non-isolated buck converter. A common protection is a "crowbar": a zener of about 6.2 V and a thyristor (SCR) across the output, which shorts the output (and blows the input fuse) if the voltage climbs too high.

    What happens if the zener fails?

    Open circuit: nothing clamps Z, so Z rises toward Q2's base-emitter clamp and the output rises until Q2 runs out of control range, potentially to near the input voltage. Short circuit: Z is held at 0 V, Q2 never turns on, and the output stays at 0 V. The first is dangerous to the load, the second only annoying.

    What happens if the input is connected backwards?

    The "+14 V" rail is now 14 V below ground. D1 (from ground up to node A) and Q1's collector-base junction (from node A up to Q1's base, which R1 ties to the rail) are both forward biased, in series, straight across the reversed supply. Nothing limits that current, so D1 or Q1 fails within moments, and if the supply can deliver amps, wiring gets hot too. The zener is also forward biased, but RZ limits its current. A series diode, or a P-MOSFET reverse-polarity protection circuit on the input, prevents all of this.

    Is it safe to use in a car?

    Not as drawn. Car electrical systems produce "load dump" transients of 40 V or more when a battery connection is broken while the alternator is charging, plus fast spikes from ignition and motors. Those can exceed Q1's and D1's ratings. Automotive designs add a TVS diode across the input, an input filter, reverse-polarity protection, current limiting, and parts rated for at least 60 V. As a learning circuit on a bench supply, it is fine.

    Will it interfere with radios?

    It can. Every switching edge contains harmonics extending into the MHz range, and node A's fast 14 V swing is a strong source. Keep the hot loop small, keep node A's copper area small, use a shielded (closed-core) inductor, add input filtering, and put the circuit in a metal box if it powers anything with an antenna. The varying frequency spreads the noise, which reduces peaks but makes it harder to avoid a specific frequency.

    Context and comparisons

    Who is Roman Black and why is it named after him?

    Roman Black is an electronics designer who published this circuit, along with many other minimalist designs, on his website. The drawings carry the address www.romanblack.com/smps.htm, which is the original source; it is worth reading for his own description and build notes. "Black regulator" is simply the name people use for this design, after him.

    Why build this instead of using a regulator IC like an LM2596 or MC34063?

    For a product, you usually would use an IC: it gives current limiting, thermal shutdown, soft start, a precise reference and better efficiency, in one part. The reasons to build this circuit are understanding (every function of a switching regulator is visible in discrete parts you can probe), availability (it can be built entirely from common parts in a junk box), cost, and the satisfaction of a working switching supply with two transistors. The MC34063, incidentally, uses a related idea: a fixed oscillator but a simple comparator-gated switch rather than PWM.

    Is this the same as a "hysteretic" or "bang-bang" regulator?

    It belongs to the same family. A pure hysteretic regulator switches on below one output voltage and off above another. This one has one threshold plus a timed off-period, so the more precise name is a constant off-time (COT) regulator. All of these are "ripple-based" controls: they react directly to the output ripple each cycle, rather than averaging an error over many cycles as a PWM regulator does.

    How is it different from a regular PWM buck controller?

    A PWM controller has a fixed-frequency clock, an error amplifier that compares the output to a reference and slowly integrates the difference, and a modulator that sets each cycle's duty from the amplifier output. That loop needs careful compensation to stay stable, and responds to load changes over several cycles. A ripple regulator like this one has no error amplifier and no compensation, is inherently stable in its own way, and reacts within one cycle, but has a variable frequency and depends on the output ripple to work.

    Why not just use a linear regulator and a heatsink?

    At 14 V to 5 V and 0.25 A, a 7805 would dissipate 2.25 W and draw 3.5 W from the supply. This circuit draws about 1.5 W for the same output and needs no heatsink. That matters when running from a battery, or inside a sealed box. A linear regulator is quieter and simpler, so for small currents or small voltage differences it is often the better choice.

    Why is the original input 14 V?

    Probably as a representative "nominal 12 V" supply: a car or lead-acid battery while charging sits around 13.8–14.4 V, and many unregulated 12 V wall adaptors deliver 14 V or more at light load. The circuit works over a wide range either side.

    Is this simulator the same as SPICE?

    No. SPICE solves the full nonlinear equations of every device. This simulator models each part by its key behaviour (switch, comparator, clamp) and integrates only the inductor, the output capacitor and node Z. It's good enough to show the mechanism and predict frequency, duty cycle and ripple within roughly 10–20 %, but it can't show switching losses, ringing or linear-mode behaviour. To go further, LTspice (free) has models for the BC327, BC337 and 1N5819, and this circuit is a good first SMPS simulation.

    Worked design example: choosing every value

    How the representative values on this page were picked, in the order you would pick them. Target: 14 V in, 5 V out, 250 mA nominal, 500 mA maximum, around 50–100 kHz.

    1. Zener: VZ = Vout + VBE ≈ 5 + 0.62 = 5.62 V → a standard 5.6 V part.
    2. RZ: aim for 5–10 mA zener current at the lowest input voltage you expect. (14 − 5.6) / 8.4 mA ≈ 1 kΩ. Power: 8.4 mA × 8.4 V ≈ 71 mW: 0.25 W resistor.
    3. Q1 base drive (R2): peak Q1 current is about 0.6 A at maximum load. For solid saturation, use a forced beta of 20–40: IB ≈ 15–30 mA. With 8.1 V across R2: 8.1 V / 17 mA ≈ 470 Ω.
    4. R1: small enough to pull Q1's base charge out quickly, large enough not to steal much drive: about 10× R2 or less. 1 kΩ steals 0.8 mA, under 5 % of the drive.
    5. Off-time and C2: choose a target frequency, say 85 kHz. With D ≈ 0.38, toff = (1 − D)/f ≈ 7.3 µs. Then C2 = toff · IRZ / ΔVA = 7.3 µs × 9 mA / 14.15 V ≈ 4.6 nF → 4.7 nF, 50 V, C0G or film.
    6. C1: at least 10× C2 so the kick on Z stays around a volt and well inside Q2's 5 V emitter-base limit: 47 nF. Kick = 14.15 × 4.7/51.7 ≈ 1.29 V.
    7. L1: choose the ripple current, typically 20–50 % of full load. ΔIL = (Vout + VF) · toff / L. For about 180 mA (36 % of 500 mA): L = 5.33 × 7.3 µs / 0.18 ≈ 216 µH → 220 µH, rated for Isat > 0.8 A.
    8. D1: average current up to (1 − D) × 0.5 A ≈ 0.31 A, reverse voltage 14 V: a 1N5819 (1 A, 40 V Schottky) has plenty of margin.
    9. CLOAD: ripple is dominated by ESR, so choose by ESR first: for 20 mV ripple at 180 mA, ESR ≤ 0.11 Ω. A 220 µF, 10–16 V low-ESR electrolytic typically has about 0.1 Ω. Check its ripple-current rating exceeds ΔIL/√12 ≈ 52 mA RMS (easily met).
    10. Check Q2's base: Q2 carries about 17 mA; with a gain of 200 its base needs about 0.09 mA from RZ's 8.4 mA. Ample.
    11. Check start-up: Q1's maximum current at start-up is about hFE × 28 mA. With a high-gain BC327-40 this can exceed its 800 mA rating. Accept the risk for a bench circuit, or add soft start or current limiting.

    Glossary

    Buck converter
    A switching regulator that produces a lower DC voltage than its input, using a switch, a diode (or second switch), an inductor and a capacitor.
    Catch / freewheel diode
    D1. Carries the inductor current while the switch is off.
    CCM / DCM
    Continuous / discontinuous conduction mode: whether the inductor current stays above zero all cycle, or falls to zero for part of it.
    Comparator
    A circuit whose output is either on or off depending on which of two inputs is higher. Q2 compares V(Z) − VBE with the output.
    Constant off-time (COT)
    A control method where the switch stays off for a fixed time, then on until the output reaches a threshold.
    Conventional current
    Current imagined as flowing from + to −. Used on all schematics.
    CRO
    Cathode-ray oscilloscope. Now used loosely for any oscilloscope.
    DCR
    DC resistance of an inductor's winding.
    Duty cycle (D)
    The fraction of each switching period the switch is on.
    ESR
    Equivalent series resistance of a capacitor. Converts ripple current into ripple voltage.
    Forced beta
    Collector current divided by the base current actually supplied. Well below hFE means the transistor is saturated.
    hFE (β)
    A bipolar transistor's DC current gain: collector current over base current in the active region.
    High-side switch
    A switch between the positive supply and the load (as opposed to low-side, between load and ground).
    Hot loop
    The physical loop carrying sharp-edged current: input capacitor, Q1, D1, ground. Keep it small.
    Hysteresis
    Different switching thresholds depending on direction, to prevent chatter.
    Inrush
    The large current at power-on while capacitors charge.
    Isat
    The current at which an inductor's core saturates and its inductance drops sharply.
    Node
    A set of connected conductors, all at one voltage.
    Positive feedback
    Feedback that reinforces a change, making a circuit snap between states.
    Reverse recovery
    The time a conducting PN diode takes to stop conducting when reversed. Schottky diodes have essentially none.
    Ripple
    The small AC variation riding on a DC output, at the switching frequency.
    Saturation (BJT)
    A transistor driven fully on, with minimal collector-emitter voltage.
    Saturation (inductor)
    A different thing: the core can hold no more magnetic flux and the inductance collapses.
    Schmitt trigger
    A comparator with built-in hysteresis.
    Schottky diode
    A metal-semiconductor diode with low forward voltage and no reverse-recovery charge.
    Storage time
    The delay before a saturated bipolar transistor starts turning off.
    Switch node
    Node A: the junction of switch, diode and inductor, which swings the full supply.
    VBE
    Base-emitter voltage. About 0.6–0.7 V when a silicon transistor conducts.
    VCE(sat)
    Collector-emitter voltage of a saturated transistor.
    VEBO
    The maximum reverse voltage a transistor's emitter-base junction can take, about 5 V for small transistors.
    Volt-second balance
    In steady state, the inductor's average voltage is zero: (V across it when on) × ton = (V across it when off) × toff.
    Zener diode
    A diode meant to conduct at a defined reverse voltage, used as a voltage reference.
